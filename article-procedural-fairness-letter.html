The Procedural Fairness Letter: Why You Must Not Ignore It

By Bansal Law · Barristers & Solicitors, Brampton, ON

What Is a Procedural Fairness Letter?

A procedural fairness letter (PFL) is a formal communication from IRCC advising you that the officer processing your application has concerns — concerns that, if unaddressed, will likely result in a refusal. It is IRCC's mechanism for meeting one of the foundational requirements of Canadian administrative law: before making a decision that negatively affects you, the decision-maker must give you a meaningful opportunity to know the case against you and respond to it.

A PFL is not routine correspondence. It is not a request for missing documents. It is a signal — sometimes a warning, sometimes a near-final notice — that a refusal is imminent unless you respond effectively.

Why Procedural Fairness Is a Legal Requirement

The duty of procedural fairness in administrative decision-making is constitutionally grounded in the principles of fundamental justice under section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, and is embedded in the common law. In immigration law, the specific content of the duty varies depending on the nature of the decision and its consequences.

For most immigration decisions with significant consequences — misrepresentation findings, inadmissibility determinations, long-term status impacts — the duty of procedural fairness requires that the applicant be told specifically what the concern is, given a genuine opportunity to respond, and have that response genuinely considered before the final decision is made.

A failure to provide procedural fairness — including issuing a PFL that is too vague to respond to, or refusing without considering the response — is a ground for judicial review.

What a PFL Typically Looks Like

PFLs vary in specificity. Some are precise: "The officer has concerns about whether your employment history as described on your application accurately reflects your actual work experience." Others are frustratingly vague: "The officer has concerns about the genuineness of your application."

In both cases, the letter will set a deadline — typically 30 days, though this varies. The letter will ask you to submit a response addressing the concern.

The Most Common PFL Scenarios

Misrepresentation concerns

IRCC believes that information on your application was false or misleading in a way that could affect the outcome. This is among the most serious PFL scenarios because a misrepresentation finding carries a two-year inadmissibility bar.

Relationship genuineness

Common in spousal and family class sponsorships. IRCC is not satisfied the relationship is genuine or that the marriage was not entered into primarily for immigration purposes.

Criminal or security concerns

IRCC has received information about a potential inadmissibility ground and is providing an opportunity to respond before a finding is made.

Document authenticity

IRCC has concerns about whether documents submitted (employment letters, financial records, educational credentials) are authentic.

How to Respond — And Why It Matters

The most important thing to understand about a PFL response is that it will be on the record. If IRCC refuses after your response, and you seek judicial review, the Federal Court will read both the PFL and your response. A poorly constructed response — one that doesn't actually address the specific concern, or that makes new admissions without realizing their consequences — can damage your position not just with IRCC but in any subsequent litigation.

An effective PFL response:

  • Identifies and directly addresses each specific concern raised
  • Provides documentary evidence corroborating the response
  • Applies the correct legal framework (for example, citing the test for misrepresentation, which requires both materiality and intent)
  • Is organized, clear, and written with the possibility of judicial review in mind
  • Does not make unnecessary admissions or volunteer damaging information

The Deadline Is Real

IRCC's response deadlines in PFLs are not suggestions. If you miss the deadline, IRCC will proceed to decide the application without your response — and you will have permanently lost the opportunity to address the concern. Extensions are sometimes granted but are not guaranteed and require prompt action.

"If you've received a procedural fairness letter, contact a lawyer before you respond. A poorly constructed response can damage your case at both the IRCC stage and in any subsequent judicial review."

What Happens After You Respond?

IRCC is required to genuinely consider your response before making a final decision. "Genuinely consider" means more than reading it — the officer's final decision should engage with the specific evidence and arguments you raised. If the decision ignores your response entirely, or addresses it only in generic terms, that failure itself becomes a ground for judicial review.

This is why it matters how your response is structured: it sets the terms on which the final decision will be reviewed, if it comes to that.
